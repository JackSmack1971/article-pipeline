## Why Are Both the Mind and Its Institutions Now Battlespaces?

Contemporary conflict increasingly targets two distinct layers at once: individual cognition and the institutions societies rely on to establish truth. Treating these as one undifferentiated problem — "disinformation" — obscures who is actually being targeted and what kind of defense each layer requires.

**Cognitive warfare** targets the psychological and behavioral processes through which individuals perceive, reason, and decide. **Epistemic warfare** operates one level up, contesting the institutions, models, and knowledge systems societies use to adjudicate what counts as true.

The two are mutually reinforcing but not interchangeable. A campaign that manipulates an individual's biases is a different problem, with different remedies, than a campaign that erodes trust in the courts, universities, or election administrators who certify facts for everyone else. This article treats that distinction as the organizing question, tracing each layer's doctrine, evidence, and open disputes before turning to what an effective response requires.

### How Should Contested Evidence Be Presented?

The account that follows draws on military and strategic-studies literature, philosophy of science, international law, and decolonial scholarship. Where researchers disagree — and on several central questions here, they disagree sharply — this article presents both positions rather than adopting one, flagging explicitly where a tension remains genuinely unresolved.

## What Is Cognitive Warfare, and Is It a Coherent Doctrine?

Cognitive warfare is a NATO-originated doctrinal concept describing operations that target human cognition directly, rather than using information merely as a tool. Whether it names a genuinely new form of conflict or simply relabels older political-warfare theory is a live, unresolved dispute among specialists.

### The Doctrinal Case

[NATO's Allied Command Transformation formally defines cognitive warfare](https://www.act.nato.int/article/cogwar-concept/) as an activity that targets cognition itself, distinguishing it from classic information warfare and psychological-operations doctrine, where messaging is a means to another end. NATO's Innovation Hub work goes further, stating that in cognitive warfare, [the brain "is not the means, it is the fight itself"](https://www.act.nato.int/article/cognitive-warfare-beyond-military-information-support-operations/) — an explicit claim that the target has shifted from behavior to cognition as such.

This is not a fringe position within the alliance. [The foundational doctrine paper reflects contributions from more than 20 NATO member nations](https://innovationhub-act.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/CW-article-Claverie-du-Cluzel-final_0.pdf), spanning commands, academia, and industry partners. A 2024 peer-reviewed conceptual analysis, however, [characterizes the concept as still-consolidating](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/big-data/articles/10.3389/fdata.2024.1452129/full) rather than settled — institutional buy-in and conceptual maturity are not the same thing.

### Why Do Skeptics Reject the Doctrine?

The strongest challenge comes from within strategic-studies scholarship itself. [A peer-reviewed analysis in the European Journal of International Security argues](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/european-journal-of-international-security/article/cognitive-warfare-some-sceptical-observations-from-general-strategic-theory-and-affective-science/2D1C2A97BDA7BF09F9FFCC7238A82709) that the concept "misrepresents the nature of the challenge," drawing on general strategic theory and affective science to question whether cognition can be isolated as a discrete target at all.

Practitioner criticism runs parallel. [One analysis argues the term adds little beyond decades-old political-warfare theory](https://smallwarsjournal.com/2025/11/18/cognitive-warfare-fails-the-cognitive-test/), and a separate assessment goes further, [calling attribution of any real-world outcome to "cognitive warfare" effectively impossible](https://www.irregularwarfare.org/is-cognitive-warfare-dead-on-arrival/) given the difficulty of isolating its causal effect from ordinary persuasion, propaganda, and politics. Some securitization scholars have proposed reframing the activity as "subversion" instead, arguing the newer label obscures more than it clarifies (ETH Zurich Center for Security Studies, 2026).

Both positions rest on serious scholarship, and neither has resolved the other. This article treats "cognitive warfare" as NATO's working doctrinal term throughout, while flagging that its status as a genuinely novel category — rather than a rebranded one — remains contested.

### Does the Individual/Structural Distinction Itself Hold Up?

A political-science analysis of NATO's concept, presented to [NATO's own Science and Technology Organization](https://publications.sto.nato.int/publications/STO%20Meeting%20Proceedings/STO-MP-HFM-361/MP-HFM-361-P13.pdf), warns that cognitive-warfare definitions are prone to "conceptual stretching" — expanding to cover so much that the boundary between individual-level cognitive targeting and structural-level institutional targeting blurs in practice, even where it holds analytically.

That finding cuts toward this article's own organizing distinction, not just NATO's. The cognitive-warfare/epistemic-warfare split used throughout this piece is best read as an analytical device for isolating distinct harms and remedies, not a claim that the two layers are operationally separable in every real-world campaign — a narrower claim than the doctrinal literature's own boundary-drawing has managed to sustain.

## How Does Cognitive Warfare Target the Brain?

At the individual level, cognitive warfare works primarily by exploiting well-documented cognitive biases, not by deploying speculative brain-hacking technology. Separating the two is essential to assessing the threat accurately.

### The Present: Bias Exploitation

A peer-reviewed analysis of the Russia-Ukraine conflict [identifies confirmation bias, anchoring, and narrative-coherence exploitation](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/artificial-intelligence/articles/10.3389/frai.2025.1566022/full) as the operative mechanism through which cognitive-warfare campaigns influence audiences — techniques closer to sophisticated marketing than to neuroscience. NATO itself links the doctrine to [convergent advances in neuroscience, artificial intelligence, and other emerging technologies](https://www.act.nato.int/activities/cognitive-warfare/), and its Innovation Hub has proposed a "Human Domain" concept built explicitly on what it calls the [militarisation of brain sciences](https://natoassociation.ca/the-battle-for-the-brain-cognitive-warfare-is-already-here/). The proposal drew enough attention that the [European Parliament formally pressed the European Commission](https://www.europarl.europa.eu/doceo/document/E-9-2022-001093_EN.html) on NATO-linked brain-sciences research in 2022.

### Are Neuroweapons Operational Yet?

Direct neurotechnological weapons are a different matter, and the evidence for their operational readiness is thin. Defense-university assessments have repeatedly found that [neuroweapons technology is not yet mature for operational employment](https://ndupress.ndu.edu/Portals/68/Documents/prism/prism_8-3/prism_8-3_DeFranco-DiEuliis-Giordano_48-63.pdf), and specialist analysis goes further, [rejecting as technically unrealistic](https://thebulletin.org/2016/10/when-neuroscience-leads-to-neuroweapons/) the idea of a precisely targeted, genuinely nonlethal neuroweapon given real-world dosing and environmental variability.

Even researchers sympathetic to the field concede a structural complication: [neuroscience research is fundamentally dual-use](https://www.bakerinstitute.org/research/neuroweapons-neuroshields-safeguarding-brain-capital-national-security), which makes drawing a clean line around a distinct "neuroweapons" category difficult. The doctrinally supported claim — that biases are being exploited at scale — should not be conflated with the speculative claim that brains are being directly weaponized; the current evidence supports only the former.

## What Is Epistemic Warfare?

Epistemic warfare operates one structural level above cognitive warfare. Rather than targeting an individual's reasoning, it contests or degrades the institutions, models, and authorities a society relies on to establish shared truth and coordinate collective action.

### From Individual Targets to Institutional Ones

The [Centre for Emerging Technology and Security (CETaS) at the Alan Turing Institute, working with the think tank Demos, defines epistemic security](https://cetas.turing.ac.uk/publications/epistemic-security-crisis-resilience) as a society's collective capacity to sustain reliable information pipelines — a structural, systems-level concept rather than a content-moderation one. That research [ties information-pipeline integrity directly to a society's capacity to navigate crises](https://cetas.turing.ac.uk/about), treating epistemic health as infrastructure rather than as a matter of individual media literacy.

Philosophy-of-science scholarship supplies the conceptual backbone for this distinction. Work building on [Lorenzo Magnani's philosophy of science distinguishes epistemic warfare's targeting of knowledge-validation systems](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-319-59256-5_4) — peer review, expert credentialing, journalistic verification — from information warfare's narrower focus on message content. On this account, an epistemic-warfare campaign succeeds not by convincing any single person of a falsehood, but by degrading the process a society uses to sort truth from falsehood in the first place.

### Why Does International Law Fall Short Here?

This structural framing collides with a legal gap. [Peer-reviewed legal scholarship confirms there is no settled international agreement](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/23311886.2026.2687180) that disinformation alone triggers the thresholds that define armed conflict under international law. The [International Committee of the Red Cross (ICRC) has stated](https://blogs.icrc.org/law-and-policy/2026/02/05/cognitive-warfare-why-the-human-brain-should-not-become-a-battlefield/) that cognitive operations complicate traditional international humanitarian law (IHL) concepts of "attack" and "harm" precisely because they target the psychological domain without crossing a kinetic threshold.

Legal scholars are similarly unsettled on the UN Charter question: [analysis published on EJIL:Talk! concludes that whether cognitive warfare constitutes "prohibited force"](https://www.ejiltalk.org/cognitive-warfare-does-it-constitute-prohibited-force/) under the Charter remains an open legal question — the threshold that, if crossed, would let a targeted state legally invoke armed self-defense — and separate peer-reviewed law scholarship frames [neurotechnology-enabled cognitive warfare as a novel challenge](https://www.elgaronline.com/view/journals/cilj/14/2/article-p185.xml) current international law was not designed to address. The doctrine has, in effect, outpaced the legal categories meant to govern it.

## Is Epistemic Security a Neutral Framework or a New Site of Power?

Framing epistemic problems in security terms is not itself a neutral technical choice. A substantial body of scholarship argues that the security frame carries its own political costs, independent of how severe the underlying problem actually is — and this article's own recommendation to strengthen "epistemic security" is not exempt from that critique, not merely adjacent to it.

### The Case for Epistemic Security as Protection

Proponents present epistemic security as a defensive, structural response: protecting the "healthy information pipelines" institutions need to function, without dictating which specific claims are true. On this account, strengthening fact-checking capacity, source verification, and institutional transparency is analogous to strengthening any other piece of public infrastructure.

### Does Security Framing Concentrate Power Instead?

A peer-reviewed security-studies analysis of the COVID-19 lab-leak controversy shows the opposite dynamic in practice: [epistemic-security language was used to police the boundary](https://academic.oup.com/sd/article/57/1/84/8413702) between legitimate inquiry and "conspiracy," in a case where the disfavored hypothesis later gained substantial credibility.

One public-philosophy commentary — a less rigorously vetted source than the peer-reviewed work cited elsewhere in this section, included because it names the dynamic sharply — frames this as an ["epistemic state of exception,"](https://thephilosophicalsalon.com) warning that security framings can exclude dissenting voices from legitimate knowledge-production. Legal scholarship adds that defending expression requires grounding speech norms in cooperative truth-seeking, not security logic, which tends toward restriction by default ("The Risks of Speech in Times of Epistemic Assault," ScienceDirect).

The theoretical foundation for this critique is Copenhagen School securitization theory, which holds that [it is the act of framing an issue as a security matter — not the issue's underlying severity — that drives the resulting curtailment of civil liberties and concentration of authority](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/00108367251382793). Applied to civilian problems like polarization or platform design, critics warn that "warfare" framing risks [miscategorizing what may be ordinary commercial byproducts as adversary action](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/european-journal-of-international-security/article/cognitive-warfare-some-sceptical-observations-from-general-strategic-theory-and-affective-science/2D1C2A97BDA7BF09F9FFCC7238A82709).

### An Unresolved Tension, Not a Resolved One

Securitization scholarship documents that past emergency framings — around COVID-19, climate change, and the war in Ukraine — [concentrated narrative control in security institutions](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/00108367251382793) rather than distributing it more broadly. The same dynamic could, in principle, apply to epistemic security itself: a framework built to protect pluralism could end up concentrating the authority to decide what counts as legitimate knowledge in the hands of whichever institutions get to declare the emergency.

This is a genuine, unresolved tension internal to this article's own thesis, not a rhetorical concession. Nothing in the evidence reviewed here settles whether epistemic-security frameworks can be built to avoid the self-undermining pattern securitization theory predicts — it remains the central open question this article returns to in its conclusion.

## How Effective Is Manufactured Doubt, From Tobacco to Elections?

Institutional-credibility attacks are not new; the tactics recur across decades and target types with a consistent structure. What is genuinely uncertain, and often overstated, is how effective those campaigns actually are at changing what people believe.

### A Recurring Template

The historical template dates at least to 1969, when [a Brown & Williamson tobacco-industry memo declared "doubt is our product"](https://www.phcppros.com/articles/367-doubt-is-our-product), explicitly naming manufactured uncertainty — not persuasion toward a specific alternative claim — as the strategic goal. The [Union of Concerned Scientists documents a recurring multi-tactic pattern](https://www.ucs.org/resources/disinformation-playbook) across later controversies, from tobacco to climate science, though the specific tactics used vary by case rather than following one fixed template.

The pattern extends beyond corporate actors. Reporting has found that a senior [Western States Petroleum Association lobbyist secretly ran more than a dozen front groups](https://www.ucs.org/resources/how-fossil-fuel-lobbyists-used-astroturf-front-groups-confuse-public) opposing climate policy, and the [Brennan Center documents sustained, coordinated harassment of U.S. election officials](https://www.brennancenter.org/our-work/research-reports/election-deniers-playbook-2024), with 45% of surveyed officials reporting fear for colleagues' safety following the 2024 election cycle.

### Do These Campaigns Actually Change Minds?

Whether these campaigns reliably change beliefs at scale is far less settled than their existence. A [systematic review of 82 studies conducted between 1995 and 2020 found limited evidence](https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2021/06/measuring-the-effects-of-influence-operations-key-findings-and-gaps-from-empirical-research) that influence operations actually shift strongly held beliefs or behavior, and a [2023 replication study found no backfire effect](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0281140) when correcting vaccine misinformation — undercutting an earlier, widely cited finding that corrections could entrench false beliefs further.

RAND research adds a caution that cuts against strong claims in either direction: [disinformation's real-world effects remain "poorly understood and hard to quantify,"](https://www.rand.org/pubs/research_reports/RR4373z1.html) meaning confident assertions about manufactured doubt's persuasive power — including some appearing in this article's own source base — outrun what the measurement literature can currently support. The tactics are well documented; their downstream effect on belief is not.

## Is Chile's Neurorights Model a Precedent Worth Following?

Chile's constitutional neurorights reform is the most concrete real-world policy response so far to cognitive-liberty concerns, and international bodies have moved quickly to build on it. Whether it is a validated template or a contested first experiment remains genuinely disputed among legal scholars — including a live dispute over whether Chile's landmark case needed a new rights category at all.

### The Policy Response

Chile became, in 2021, [the first country to enshrine neurorights constitutionally](https://courier.unesco.org/en/articles/chile-pioneering-protection-neurorights), and its Supreme Court followed in 2023 by ordering a neurotechnology company to delete a user's brain data under that new framework. That 2023 ruling is itself contested ground: [a Stanford Law School analysis reports that a Chilean law professor argued the neurorights provision was irrelevant to the outcome](https://law.stanford.edu/2026/04/27/even-chiles-neurorights-leave-inferred-mental-data-in-a-gray-zone/), since the company would have had to delete the data under Chile's existing personal-data-protection law — modeled on the European Union's General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) — regardless.

Neurolaw scholarship has since formalized **cognitive liberty** as one of four core neurorights categories, alongside mental privacy, mental integrity, and psychological continuity, treated as a negative right against interference (Visegrad Journal on Human Rights).

The precedent moved quickly to the international level: in November 2025, [the General Conference of the United Nations Educational, Scientific and Cultural Organization (UNESCO) adopted the first global neurotechnology-ethics standard](https://sites.uab.edu/humanrights/2025/11/11/neurorights-and-mental-privacy/), with 194 member states signing on. Researchers at CETaS and Demos have proposed [strengthening societal information pipelines as the structural counterpart](https://cetas.turing.ac.uk/publications/epistemic-security-crisis-resilience) to this individual-level cognitive-liberty protection — pairing the two layers this article distinguishes throughout.

### Is Chile's Model Legally Justified?

Legal scholars are considerably less settled on whether the Chilean model was the right instrument. One line of legal scholarship (Moreu Carbonell) argues Chile's reform is poorly justified, because existing privacy and bodily-integrity rights frameworks may already cover the harms neurorights law was designed to address — making the new category legally redundant rather than necessary.

Peer-reviewed neuroethics scholarship goes further, documenting [unintended consequences flowing from the reform's negative-rights framing](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12152-022-09504-z), which it argues is poorly matched to the actual structure of neurotechnological harm. A 2025 medical-informatics and ethics analysis confirms the dispute is live rather than settled, reporting that [Chilean legal experts themselves remain split](https://www.jmir.org/2025/1/e72270) on whether the new neurorights law was needed at all. Chile's reform is real and binding, but whether it should be replicated elsewhere is an open legal question, not a settled best practice.

## Whose Knowledge Counts? Epistemic Injustice and the Risk of Relativism

Philosophy offers a framework for a harm distinct from cognitive or epistemic warfare as doctrinally defined: injustice done to a person specifically in their capacity as a knower. That framework extends usefully to structural power, but applying it indiscriminately carries its own risk.

### Fricker's Framework and Its Decolonial Extension

Philosopher [Miranda Fricker distinguishes testimonial injustice](https://ndpr.nd.edu/reviews/epistemic-injustice-power-and-the-ethics-of-knowing/) — when someone's credibility is unfairly deflated because of prejudice against their social identity — from hermeneutical injustice, where structural gaps in shared interpretive resources leave a group unable to make sense of their own experience. Peer-reviewed work in *Episteme* has [directly examined whether Fricker's account can be extended to support decoloniality](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/episteme/article/can-frickerian-accounts-of-epistemic-justice-promote-decoloniality-a-critical-examination/9EC61243CEFD835AA9C166D89BCBA3E1), applying the framework to colonial and postcolonial knowledge hierarchies.

The most recent scholarship on that question is notably skeptical: Ranjoo Seodu Herr's 2025 analysis [concludes that Frickerian accounts of epistemic justice cannot, on their own, promote decoloniality](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/episteme/article/can-frickerian-accounts-of-epistemic-justice-promote-decoloniality-a-critical-examination/9EC61243CEFD835AA9C166D89BCBA3E1), revising an earlier framing of the project as a successful extension. The philosophical tool illuminates a real harm; it does not, by itself, resolve the political project some hoped to build on it.

### Does Epistemic-Injustice Language Risk Fueling Relativism?

Applying epistemic-injustice language indiscriminately carries a documented cost. Peer-reviewed political-communication research has found that delegitimization campaigns now [extend to misinformation researchers themselves](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/10584609.2025.2514595), with attacks on their credibility risking a broader spread of the belief that facts are simply relative to perspective.

Scholarship on epistemic populism has examined whether "common sense versus expert" framing measurably increases that perceived relativism; the cited experimental work found [broader erosion of trust in expertise but not a statistically significant increase in perceived factual relativism specifically](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-031-64178-7_2) — a distinction worth preserving rather than collapsing into a single alarming headline. Epistemic-injustice claims, deployed carelessly, risk validating exactly the "your truth versus my truth" framing that epistemic warfare's institutional-erosion tactics depend on.

## Toward Conceptual Precision and Resilient Pluralism

This article has argued for a distinction, not a hierarchy: cognitive warfare targets individual perception and decision-making, largely through documented bias exploitation rather than speculative neuroweapons; epistemic warfare targets the institutions and knowledge-validation systems societies depend on to establish truth collectively. Existing military doctrine and international law address neither cleanly, leaving both without settled legal or conceptual footing.

The evidence reviewed here supports building toward two things: protecting **cognitive liberty** at the individual level, following — critically, not uncritically — Chile's contested constitutional precedent, and strengthening epistemic security at the institutional level, following CETaS's "healthy information pipelines" model. Neither response is validated as sufficient, and the Chilean model in particular remains disputed among the legal scholars who study it most closely.

The deeper problem this article cannot resolve is the one raised in the epistemic-security section: security framing itself, securitization theory suggests, tends to concentrate authority and narrow legitimate dissent regardless of the severity of the underlying threat. A response to epistemic warfare built entirely in security terms risks reproducing, in defense of pluralism, the same concentration of interpretive authority that epistemic warfare exploits when practiced by adversaries.

That tension has no clean resolution in the current literature. Treating it as an open question to be managed, rather than a problem already solved by adopting a security frame, is this article's actual claim.
