# Article Specification

## Thesis
Cognitive warfare and epistemic warfare should be understood not as interchangeable labels or wholly novel forms of conflict, but as distinct yet mutually reinforcing dimensions of a broader struggle over contested cognition: cognitive warfare targets the psychological, behavioral, and increasingly neurotechnological processes through which individuals perceive, reason, and decide, while epistemic warfare operates at the structural level by contesting or manipulating the institutions, authorities, models, and knowledge systems through which societies establish truth and coordinate collective action. Integrating military and strategic studies with political science, science and technology studies, philosophy, law, and decolonial scholarship reveals that the central security challenge is therefore not merely adversarial information, but the systematic exploitation of vulnerabilities across the entire knowledge-production environment. Because existing military doctrine, international law, and content-focused countermeasures inadequately capture these interconnected forms of non-kinetic harm, an effective response requires greater conceptual precision and a shift toward protecting cognitive liberty, strengthening epistemic security, and building resilient socio-technical and institutional systems capable of sustaining human agency, pluralism, and democratic decision-making under conditions of persistent epistemic contestation.

## Target Audience
Educated general-interest readers of serious policy/foreign-affairs publications — familiar with terms like "disinformation" and "information warfare," but not with NATO doctrinal vocabulary, securitization theory, neurorights law, or epistemic-injustice philosophy. No prior specialist knowledge assumed; all technical terms defined on first use.
